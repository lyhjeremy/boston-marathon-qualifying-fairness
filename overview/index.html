Boston BQ fairness
22 brackets, three frameworks

Boston Marathon qualifying times, 2026

Fair to women on average. Badly uneven bracket to bracket.

The BAA sets a qualifying time for each of 22 age-gender brackets and has never published how. I scored every standard against three references: the bracket's world record, an averaged top three, and WMA age grading. Men and women are asked for the same average effort (Welch t-test, p = 0.81). The women's standards vary far more from bracket to bracket than the men's, and W80+ is about 57 minutes stricter than the rest of the table.

Start: the question

Nobody outside the BAA knows how the standards are set

Boston is one of only two World Marathon Majors that you enter by running a qualifying time. The BAA publishes a standard for 11 age groups in each of two genders. Its announcements cite "careful analysis of results data" and stop there.

So "are they fair?" can only be answered by proxy. Pick a reference for how hard each bracket should be, then check whether every standard sits at the same distance from it. Any single reference is arguable, so I used three.

  1. 1
    World-record multiplier. The BQ time divided by the bracket's world record. Transparent, and exposed to one exceptional record.
  2. 2
    Top-three records. The same ratio against an averaged top three, to see whether a single performance drives the result. The second and third marks are estimated, not looked up.
  3. 3
    WMA age grading. The share of age-specific potential each standard demands, from the WMA 2023 factor tables.

First result

The gender-bias claim does not hold

p = 0.81

Welch t-test on the gender means, world-record framework. t = −0.25.

Age grading gives the same answer. The men's standards ask for an average of 69.1% of age-graded potential and the women's for 68.3% (t = 0.42, p = 0.68). The usual forum complaint is about the mean, and on the mean the two genders are treated alike.

The result that holds

Men's standards are consistent. Women's are not.

Every men's bracket falls between 1.43× and 1.53× its world record, a coefficient of variation of 1.9%. The women's run from 1.27× to 1.62×, a CV of 6.6%. Levene's test for equal variance gives W = 5.04, p = 0.036.

W35–39 at 1.62× is the most lenient bracket in the table and W80+ at 1.27× the strictest. Two women who both meet their standard can be running at very different fractions of what their age group has shown is possible.

The gap survives a change of reference. With an averaged top three, the CVs are 2.1% and 7.3%; with age grading, 4.0% and 7.8%. The ratio is about 3.5 to 1 under the two record-based frameworks and about 2 to 1 under age grading.

The outlier

W80+ is too strict under every framework

The W80+ standard is 5:20:00. Applying the median multiplier would put it at 6:17:20, 57 minutes slower. Age grading puts the gap at 56 minutes. The two frameworks start from different premises and land a minute apart.

The reference explains it. Yoko Nakano's 4:11:45, run in Tokyo at 81, is an exceptional record, and the current standard asks women over 80 to finish within 27% of it. Every other bracket sits between 1.42× and 1.62× its record.

The 2026 tightening

Five minutes off every standard under 60

For 2026 the BAA cut five minutes from every standard below age 60, its largest tightening since 1990, after 33,249 applications for about 24,000 qualifier places. The 60+ standards did not move.

A flat cut leaves the shape of the table in place. Against the same world records, the women's CV was 7.3% under the 2020–2025 standards and is 6.6% now; the men's moved from 1.8% to 1.9%. The change answered demand. It was too uniform to fix the spread.

It did make one step steeper. The jump from 55–59 to 60–64 was 15 minutes and is now 20, for men and women alike, so a qualifier gains 20 minutes of allowance on their 60th birthday.

Stress test

Is the variance gap an artifact of the records chosen?

I re-ran the world-record framework three ways: without W80+, with Sinead Diver's 2:21:34 as an alternative W40–44 reference, and with Tigst Assefa's women-only record of 2:15:41 in place of the mixed-race mark for W18–34.

The men's CV stays at 1.9% in every case. The women's is 6.6% with Diver and 6.4% with Assefa, and drops to 4.5% only when W80+ is removed. That one bracket accounts for about a third of the women's spread, and even without it the women's CV is more than twice the men's.

Limitations

What this does not show

There are 11 brackets per gender, so the tests are underpowered. Levene's p = 0.036 clears 0.05 by a small margin, and it was run on the world-record framework only. A p of 0.81 means no difference was detected, which is weaker than evidence of no difference.

The top-three framework is an approximation. The second and third marks are set at 3% and 6% slower than the record for brackets with depth, and 5% and 10% for 70+, rather than taken from results.

Below 40 there is no separate masters record, so 18–34 and 35–39 share the open record. That makes 35–39 look more lenient than it may be.

Non-binary athletes (110 accepted in 2026) are left out because the BAA says it does not yet have the data to set standards for the category. And "fair" here means equal proportional difficulty against a reference. The BAA may be aiming at field size, continuity or something else; a different definition could reach a different verdict on the same table.
